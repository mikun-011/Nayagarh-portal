About Nayagarh

Nayagarh, the headquarters of Nayagarh district in central Odisha, is a hill-ringed town known for its temples, its forests and the sweetest export Odisha has: Chhena Poda. The Rukhi hills rise to the south, the Balaram hills to the north, and the great Mahanadi runs along the edge of the district, giving it green valleys and fertile fields.

The district was carved out of the old Puri district on 1 April 1993 and covers about 3,890 sq. km. At the 2011 census it had a population of 9,62,789 and a literacy rate of 79.17 per cent. The town itself is small and friendly, about 87 km from Bhubaneswar, with around 17,000 residents living in 13 wards.

Nayagarh was once a princely state. Its rulers, the Mandhata family of the Baghela line, took a tiger’s head as their emblem. The district still remembers its four old Gajats — Nayagarh, Ranpur, Khandapada and Daspalla — whose palaces, temples and festivals shaped local life. The state joined India on 1 January 1948.

Faith runs deep here. Ladukeshwar (Ladoo Baba) at Sarankul, Raghunath Jew at Odagaon, Nilamadhab at Kantilo, the hilltop Ram temple at Fatehgarh and the Dakhinakali and Jagannath temples of Nayagarh town draw pilgrims all year. Rama Navami, Maha Shivaratri, Dola Yatra and Rath Yatra are the big days in the local calendar.

And then there is the food. Nayagarh is the birthplace of Chhena Poda, first made in Daspalla in the 1940s, and its sweet shops also tempt visitors with Arisha Pitha, Chhena Murki and Kheer Kadam. For nature there are the Kuanria Dam, the Balangi Bandha Park and the quiet Mahanadi bank at Kantilo.

The Mandhata Dynasty

Nayagarh’s story begins with a Baghela prince, Suryamani, who is said to have come to Puri on pilgrimage from Rewa and founded a kingdom here, adopting the tiger’s head as its symbol. The ninth king, Baghel Singh (1480–1510), moved the capital to the hills — legend says after he saw a rabbit overpower a dog — and the place came to be called Baghua Nayagarh, “Valorous Nayagarh”.

Under Raghunath Singh (1565–1595) the state grew to include Odagaon, Sarankul and parts of Ranpur. Before his death he divided his lands among his sons, which is how Khandapada became a separate kingdom in 1599.

Binayak Singh Mandhata (1784–1825) is remembered as a patron of the court poet Jadumani Mahapatra, and the Jagannath temple of Nayagarh is attributed to his time. The Mandhata line ruled until Krushna Chandra Singh Mandhata signed the instrument of accession to India on 1 January 1948.

Culture of Nayagarh

The culture of Nayagarh is devotional, literary and deeply rooted in village life. Temple rituals set the rhythm of the year, poets and scholars of the old royal courts left a proud literary tradition, and folk performances still bring neighbourhoods together on festival nights.

Poets & Literature

Nayagarh has given Odia literature some of its best-loved names. Local tradition says Kabisamrat Upendra Bhanja composed his masterpiece Baidehisha Vilasa at the feet of Sri Raghunath at Odagaon. Jadumani Mahapatra was court poet to Binayak Singh Mandhata, and Pathani Samanta of Khandapada wrote the astronomical treatise Siddhanta Darpana.

At Udayapur, the Banchhanidhi Pathagara library, the Sri Aurobindo Sangrahalaya and the Jadumani Sahitya Sansad were set up by the late Dasarathi Pattnaik — a quiet pilgrimage for book lovers.

Folk & Stage Traditions

Danda Yatra, Ram Leela, Duari Nata, Pala, Daskathia and Sankirtan are all part of Nayagarh’s performing traditions. Many are staged around temple festivals, with whole villages joining in as audience and chorus.

Events & Festivals

The festival calendar is full. Pana Sankranti (Mahavishuva Sankranti), Dola Yatra, Rama Navami and Rath Yatra are celebrated across the district. At Sarankul, Maha Shivaratri — locally called Jagara Yatra — fills the town around Ladoo Baba Temple. At Kantilo, Magha Saptami is the big day for Lord Nilamadhab.

Every year on 11 April, Nayagarh celebrates Chhenapoda Dibasa, honouring the sweet that made the district famous.
